# What Is the T2 Life Insurance Form?

<p><strong>Here’s the Bottom Line:</strong></p>

<p>• The T2 or T-2 form is a mailer, not an insurance company or an insurance policy<br>• Sending it back is not the same as applying for coverage<br>• Your reply can lead to a call from an insurance agent or agency<br>• The mailer does not decide your price, your approval or any waiting period<br>• Before you buy, find out the insurance company, the type of coverage, the monthly premium, any waiting period and the policy terms</p>

Did you get a card in the mail marked "T2" or "T-2" about burial or final expense benefits? Here's what that form is, what can happen if you mail it back, and how to judge a real insurance offer.

## What is the T2 life insurance form?

The T2 form is a mailer about final expense insurance, the small life insurance policies people buy to cover funeral costs and final bills. It asks you to send back some personal information.

The Nebraska Department of Insurance showed a real example in a 2022 presentation on misleading marketing. The card was marked "T-2" and "2020 BENEFIT INFORMATION FOR NEBRASKA CITIZENS ONLY." It said Nebraska residents had access to a "state-regulated program" that would pay final expenses up to $35,000.

Here's what the Department found when it looked into that card:

- It came from a lead-generation service.
- Its goal was to get people to reply with their personal information.
- That information could then be sold to insurance agents.
- An agent was using the card to market several different plans, not one specific plan.

We found regulator records showing "T-2" on this kind of mailer. We did not find an official source that explains what the "T-2" code itself stands for.

## What the T2 form is not

- **It's not an insurance company.** We found no evidence of an insurance company named T2.
- **It's not an insurance policy.** Nothing on the card gives you coverage.
- **It's not an application.** Sending it back doesn't apply you for a policy with any insurance company.
- **It's not a government benefit.** In the regulator examples below, these offers were insurance marketing, not a state program. We found no state program that pays $35,000 for funerals because of your age.

## Why the mailer can look official

These mailers are often made to look like government paperwork.

In one case, the Iowa Insurance Division said an insurance salesperson picked a template mailer to send to older consumers. The templates had markings that looked like official government documents. One said the reader might qualify for a "state-regulated program" paying final expenses up to $35,000. No insurance company was named on it.

Washington, D.C.'s insurance department, the Department of Insurance, Securities and Banking (DISB), warned about a similar mailer. Its form was titled "F-1 2020 BENEFIT INFORMATION FOR DISTRICT OF COLUMBIA RESIDENTS ONLY." That's a different code than T-2, but it's the same kind of mailer. DISB said:

- The mailer was not from the District government.
- It was not a chance to get District government-sponsored funeral insurance.
- It was not sanctioned by DISB or the District government.

## What "state-regulated" really means

Life insurance and the people who sell it are regulated by each state. So any real policy you buy has to follow your state's insurance laws.

But the words "state-regulated program" on a mailer don't mean your state created, pays for or sponsors a special funeral benefit. The D.C. warning makes that point plainly. You can read more in our guide to [state-regulated life insurance](/burial-insurance/state-regulated-life-insurance/).

## What the form asks for

The D.C. example asked for:

- your name
- your spouse's name
- your age
- your spouse's age
- your phone number

It also urged people to send it back within five days.

## What happens if you send it back

Not every mailer works the same way. But the regulator examples show what can happen.

In the Iowa case, the cards went back to a company called Direct Mail Processing. People who returned a card got a telemarketing call from the insurance salesperson. Rehg Data Assets, the company the salesperson used for the mailers, was paid for each lead. The Iowa Insurance Division issued a cease-and-desist order over the mailings.

D.C.'s DISB said the information people send back may be used by, or sold to, insurance companies. Then insurance agents may contact you to try to sell you a policy.

So in these examples, sending the card back led to a sales call, not to a benefit.

## The mailer, the agent and the insurance company

It helps to know who does what:

| Who | What they do |
|---|---|
| The mailer or lead company | Collects your name and contact details |
| The insurance agent or agency | Contacts you and offers a policy |
| The insurance company | Issues the policy, sets the price and pays the claim |

Only an insurance company can issue a policy. The mailer can't.

## Does sending it back get you coverage or a set price?

No. The mailer is not an application, so it can't approve you or lock in a price.

What you'd pay, whether you're approved, and whether the policy covers you fully from the first day or has a waiting period all depend on:

- the insurance company
- the policy you're offered
- your answers on the real application

Some final expense policies pay the full benefit from day one. Others, like [guaranteed issue policies](/burial-insurance/guaranteed-issue-life-insurance-for-seniors/), usually limit what they pay for a death from illness in the first two years. The mailer doesn't tell you which kind you'll be offered.

## Are regulators doing anything about these mailers?

Yes. Lead companies are not beyond the reach of state insurance regulators.

In its 2022 presentation, the Nebraska Department of Insurance said it often sees misleading contact attempts that start with lead generators. It said lead generators can use mail and other media. It listed actions regulators can take:

- cease-and-desist orders against lead generators
- administrative action
- referrals to the Attorney General

Nebraska also warned that regulators can take action against agents or companies that use leads that were generated the wrong way. And as noted above, Iowa issued a cease-and-desist order in its case.

## How to check a real insurance offer

If an agent contacts you after you send in a form, slow down and ask:

- What insurance company would issue this policy?
- What is your name, and are you licensed in my state? (You can check an agent's license with your state's insurance department.)
- What type of policy is it?
- How much is the monthly premium, and can it ever go up?
- How much will it pay when I die?
- Is there a waiting period? If so, how long, and what does it pay during that time?

Get the answers in writing, and don't let anyone rush you. D.C.'s DISB gives good advice here: don't give out personal information unless you know who is asking for it and why.

## How to get less mail like this

DMAchoice is a mail preference service from the Association of National Advertisers. When you register, it asks companies to stop sending you promotional mail if you don't already do business with them. DMAchoice estimates it cuts that kind of mail by about 80%.

It won't stop all of it:

- Companies that don't use DMAchoice won't get your request.
- It doesn't remove you from every mailing list.
- Companies you already do business with can still mail you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is T2 life insurance a real insurance company?

We found no evidence that T2 is an insurance company. The "T-2" label has shown up on a final expense mailer that, according to the Nebraska Department of Insurance, came from a lead-generation service.

### Is the T2 form from the government?

In the regulator examples we found, no. Nebraska traced its T-2 card to a lead-generation service. D.C. said a similar F-1 mailer was not from the District government and was not government-sponsored funeral insurance.

### Do I have to send the T2 form back?

No. It's a marketing mailer, not a bill or a government notice.

### I already sent it back. What now?

You may get a call from an insurance agent or agency. You don't have to buy anything. If you're interested in coverage, ask the questions above, find out which insurance company would issue the policy, and compare more than one option before you decide.
