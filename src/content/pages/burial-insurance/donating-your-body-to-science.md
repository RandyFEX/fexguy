<h2 id="whole-body-donation-in-the-united-states"><br><strong>Whole Body Donation In The United States</strong> </h2>

<p>Body donation, also called an anatomical gift, or body bequest, is the donation of the entire body after death for education and research. Donated bodies are used to study gross anatomy and surgical anatomy and continue medical education.</p>

<p>For many years, only medical schools have accepted whole body donations. Today, private programs are also accepting whole bodies for donations. There are many private whole-body donation programs in the US. Most of these programs accept bodies from their surrounding areas.</p>

<p>There is generally no cost to donate a body to science. Most programs cover the cremation cost once the remains have served its purpose and returned the ashes to the families.</p>

<p>The Uniform Anatomical Gift Act governs a person’s legal right to choose whole body donation. Most states largely adopt it. </p>

<p>Many states instituted programs and regulations regarding
body parts and whole body donation. United States House Bill 5318 indicates
that Health and Human Services would oversee the body donation industry.</p>

<p>Any person planning on donating his body to science may do so through a willed body program. The donor is required to make a prior arrangement or pre-registration with the local medical school, university, or whole-body donation program before death. He needs to sign a consent form and know the information about the policies and procedures that will happen to their body when they die.</p>

<p>Donors must decide to donate their remains after death, and your power of attorney cannot make the decision for you. Only the legal next-of-kin of the donor could provide the necessary consent for the donation if the deceased did not submit it to the accepting facility after death.</p>

<p>Many medical programs in the US today hold student-led memorial
services for the corpses of body donation to show respect for the donors and
their families.</p>

<h2 id="types-of-body-donation"><br><strong>Types Of Body Donation</strong> </h2>

<p><strong>ORGAN AND TISSUE DONATION</strong></p>

<p>Organ and tissue donation is a great way to help others. You can donate your organs or tissue only. Your family can keep your body to bury or cremate. If you wish to be an organ donor, the next time you renew your driver’s license, check the box that says “organ donor.”</p>

<p><br><strong>WHOLE BODY DONATION</strong></p>

<p>Nearly every medical school or college of medicine has a full body donation program, which is also called the willed body program. Whole-body donation is the best way for individuals who want to donate their whole bodies and avoid the cost of a funeral. There are whole-body programs in the US to assist institutions and organizations. </p>

<p>Medical schools and independent donation programs can provide free transportation, a copy of the death certificate, cremation, and return of the ashes to the family.</p>

<p>To find whole body donation programs near you, consult the University of Florida’s list of U.S. body <a href="https://anatbd.acb.med.ufl.edu/usprograms/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">donation programs</a>. </p>

<p><br><strong>BODY FARM</strong></p>

<p>You can donate your whole body to a “body farm”. If you choose this option, your family cannot have your remains back. There are seven body farms in the US. Body farms like the University of Tennessee’s Forensic Anthropology Center will use your body to teach students about the various states of decomposition. </p>

<p>Body farms are areas of land where donated bodies are laid to be studied for human decomposition. Scientists and researchers will put the donated bodies so that they can explore the effects of environment and trauma on decomposition. All the costs associated with the donation are covered by the body farm except the transportation of the body to the facility.</p>

<p><br><strong>PLASTINATION</strong></p>

<p>Whole-body can be donated and preserved using the plastination procedure. The donated body is preserved by replacing bodily fluids and fats with hardening plastic. The bodies are hardened with heat, gas, and light. </p>

<p>The whole body donation program is currently managed by the Institute for Plastination in Heidelberg, Germany, and is on display in different parts of the world. They are not accepting new body donations because they are at full capacity, but you can check back in as time progresses.</p>

<h2 id="how-does-donating-your-body-to-science-work"><br><strong>How Does Donating Your Body To Science Really Work? </strong></h2>

<p>How do you donate your body to science? Donating your body to science is an easy process. Here are the ways:</p>

<p><strong>STEP 1: LOOK FOR A BODY DONATION PROGRAM</strong></p>

<p>Many universities and medical schools have whole body donation programs. You can also find some independent donation programs throughout the country.</p>

<p>Look for body donation programs near your area to make the transportation of the body easier. We created a list of resources down below to help you locate a program you can register to.</p>

<p><br><strong>STEP 2: PRE-REGISTER</strong> <strong>TO THE PROGRAM</strong></p>

<p>It’s an essential requirement for you to pre-register at your chosen program. Go to your nearest facility and complete the forms in the registration packet, including all paperwork and the consent form.</p>

<p>It depends on the program or organization you decide to donate how the registration and donation program works. Some programs require you to pre-register at least 30 days before death, while other programs may consider a donation at the time of death. </p>

<p>You must complete the donor consent form according to the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act (UAGA). These can be filled in during pre-registration or completed by the donor’s legal next of kin at the time of death.</p>

<p><br><strong>STEP 3: KNOW YOUR OBLIGATIONS</strong></p>

<p>Know the policy of the program regarding whole body donation. Many organizations will shoulder all the expenses, inclusive of the transportation of the body and cremation. Some programs do not include transportation of the body. </p>

<p>Be familiar with the policies; that way, you can make a backup plan if necessary.</p>

<p>If you choose to have an organ or tissue donation only to save someone’s life instead of a whole-body donation, you need to register with the Network For Organ Sharing. Also, check the box on organ donation when you renew your driver’s license. </p>

<p><br><strong>STEP 4: LET YOUR LOVED ONES</strong> <strong>KNOW</strong></p>

<p>It’s crucial to let your family or anybody in charge of making your funeral arrangements know about your decision regarding whole body donation. If they are unaware of this, it can cause some complications when you die.</p>

<p>Let your loved ones know that you have made the pre-registration and all the needed paperwork. Make sure that the people handling your final affairs are well aware of your decision so that they can tell the funeral director to inform the donation program about your passing away.</p>

<p><br><strong>STEP 5: AMEND YOUR WILL</strong></p>

<p>If you decide to donate your body to science, make sure you have instructions in your will. After updating your will, tell your loved ones so that they will know what to do and who to contact when you pass away.</p>

<p>It’s also a great idea to tell your doctor about your final wishes. Put your wishes in writing. These legal documents can spell out your wishes about your end-of-life medical treatment when you can no longer decide for yourself.</p>

<p><br><strong>STEP 6: MAKE A BACKUP PLAN</strong></p>

<p><strong>It’s possible that the body donation program may decline to accept your body donation; therefore, you need to be prepared and have a backup plan.</strong></p>

<p>You need to have something to cover the cost of your funeral and other final expenses. Having <a href="/the-importance-of-burial-insurance/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">burial insurance</a>, <a href="/final-expense-life-insurance-complete-guide/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">final expense</a> insurance, or funeral insurance to cover the cost of your end-of-life expenses will lift the financial burden on your family.</p>

<p>YES, medical institutions and private donation programs accept donations from all ages, locations, and ethnicities. </p>

<p>Some facilities require bodies with certain pre-existing medical conditions for specific training or research purposes.</p>

<p>Illnesses such as stroke, cancer, dementia, arthritis, and
diabetes will not disqualify you for donation. </p>

<p>Usually, the cremated remains are available within 4-6 weeks. Sometimes, it may take longer if the facility used the body for specific research. Generally, all donations are completed within 12 weeks.</p>

<p>NO, your body can’t be used for a specific study even if you request it. Medical schools and private donation organizations don’t always know what they will be working on in advance when they receive a body. </p>

<p>Forensic research is another way to donate aside from medical research. There are seven body farms in the US where that will leave your body to decompose under different conditions.</p>

<p>NO. The final disposition method of your body will not affect any of your benefits, pension, or insurance entitlements. </p>

<p>Your beneficiary will only need to present your death certificates for these purposes.</p>

<p>NO. Federal law prohibits the buying and selling of organs, tissue or body for transplant, research, or medical education. </p>

<p>No. Only organ donations are indicated on the driver’s license. You have to make a pre-registration to a medical school or private donation program for whole-body donation.</p>

<p><br><strong>Is donating my organ possible if I want whole body donation? </strong></p>

<p>Only the program you choose can determine if you can or can’t donate your tissues or organs to science.</p>

<p>Some private donation programs allow organ donation with a whole-body donation, while medical schools typically don’t. If you decide on donating both, inquire about the facility before making registration.</p>

<p>NO. Generally, the body donation program will arrange everything, from collecting your body, transporting it to the donation facility, arranging the death certificate, cremation of the body after the donation, and returning the remains to the family.</p>
