## What to do when someone dies without life insurance

If someone you love just died without life insurance, buying life insurance now isn't going to solve your problem. You've got a funeral to pay for, and you need to figure out where that money is coming from.

Before you start borrowing money, putting funeral expenses on credit cards, or asking friends and family for donations, let's see what we can do to reduce the amount of money you actually need.

Here's where I'd start:

1. **Don't sign anything yet.** Whoever signs the funeral home contract is usually the person who owes the bill. You don't have to make every decision in the first few hours.
2. **Get the prices first.** Funeral homes have to give you prices over the phone, and they have to hand you an itemized price list when you sit down to talk about arrangements. That's federal law, the FTC's [Funeral Rule](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-453).
3. **Ask about direct cremation and immediate burial by name.** They're the two lowest-cost options, and you can still hold a memorial service later.
4. **Ask what it costs to wait.** If you need a few days to pull money together, find out what the funeral home charges per day to hold the body.
5. **Look for money the person left behind.** Check for bank accounts with a payable-on-death beneficiary, joint accounts, and life insurance through an employer, union, credit union or an old policy. If bank statements show premium payments to an insurance company, follow that lead. You can also use the free [NAIC Life Insurance Policy Locator](https://eapps.naic.org/life-policy-locator/), which state insurance regulators use to search for policies after a death.
6. **Find out whether they served in the military.** Veterans can qualify for burial benefits. Look for discharge papers, often called a DD-214.

## Who is responsible for paying for the funeral?

This is where a lot of families get into trouble, so let's slow down. Deciding on the funeral, signing for the funeral and paying from the estate are three different things. The rules come from state law, not federal law, so they aren't the same everywhere.

**Who makes the arrangements.** Each state sets its own order. Many start with a person the deceased named in writing, then the spouse, adult children, parents and siblings, but the order and who qualifies differ by state. In some states, the person with the right to make the arrangements can also be responsible for the reasonable cost, so check your state's rule.

**Who signs the contract.** A funeral home contract is a personal promise to pay. If you sign it, you owe the funeral home, even if the estate pays you back later. That's why I wouldn't sign until you know where the money is coming from.

**What the estate pays.** Funeral costs can be paid from the deceased person's estate. In states that follow the Uniform Probate Code, reasonable funeral expenses get paid right after the costs of handling the estate and ahead of most other debts when there isn't enough to go around. Other states set their own order. But if the estate has no money, there's nothing to pay you back with.

**What family members owe.** The FTC says relatives generally don't have to pay a deceased person's debts out of their own pocket. There are exceptions, such as a co-signer, a surviving spouse in some states, or a state law that makes a relative responsible. If you aren't sure where you stand, ask your county probate court or a local attorney before you sign.

## Ways to pay for a funeral with no money

Here's the order I'd work through. The first options cost you the least, and the last ones cost you the most.

1. **Lower the bill first.** Direct cremation or immediate burial, with a memorial service later, costs far less than a traditional funeral with a viewing. Nothing else on this list saves you as much money. I cover how to do it below.
2. **Use the person's own money.** Payable-on-death and joint accounts usually go straight to the named person or co-owner. An account in the deceased's name alone may need an executor or a small-estate process before anyone can touch it, and that can take weeks. Ask the funeral home whether it will wait for the estate, or whether it accepts a life insurance policy assigned to it as payment.
3. **Claim the benefits they earned.** That means employer, union and fraternal life insurance, veterans benefits and the Social Security payment. I explain the government programs in the next section.
4. **Split it with family.** If several of you are chipping in, put it in writing: who signs, and how much each person pays. It's a lot easier to agree now than to argue later.
5. **Use an inherited retirement account.** If you're the named beneficiary of an IRA or 401(k), you don't owe the [10% early-withdrawal tax](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-exceptions-to-tax-on-early-distributions) on money you take out after the owner's death, even if you're under 59½. You'll still owe regular income tax, and the account company may need time to process the transfer.
6. **Ask your church, employer or community.** An online fundraiser can help, but you can't count on how much it raises or how fast, and some platforms take a fee.
7. **Ask about a payment plan.** Some funeral homes offer one. Get the total cost and the payment schedule in writing.
8. **Borrow only as a last resort.** A credit card or personal loan pays the bill fast, but the interest can follow you for years after the funeral.

I wouldn't borrow money until I'd worked through everything above it on this list.

## Government and funeral financial assistance

There's some help out there. Most of it is limited, and a lot of it only applies to certain people or places, so don't plan the funeral around money you haven't confirmed.

**Social Security.** Social Security pays a one-time [$255](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-404.390) death payment. It goes to the surviving spouse if they lived together. If there's no such spouse, it goes to a spouse or child who was already getting benefits on the person's record. Nobody else can get it, it isn't paid to the funeral home, and you have two years to apply. It won't go far, but if you qualify, claim it.

**Veterans benefits.** If the person was a veteran, this is worth checking right away. VA pays [burial allowances](https://www.va.gov/burials-memorials/veterans-burial-allowance/) to eligible survivors:

- For a death caused by military service, up to $2,000 for deaths on or after September 11, 2001.
- For other deaths on or after October 1, 2026, up to $1,032 toward burial and $1,032 toward a plot, when the veteran meets VA's eligibility rules. For deaths from October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026, the amounts are $1,002 each.

VA usually pays the allowance back to you after the funeral, so you'll still need to cover the bill first.

The bigger savings may be burial in a VA national cemetery. Veterans who didn't receive a dishonorable discharge, and certain family members, can generally be buried there. The gravesite (where space is available), opening and closing the grave, a burial liner, a government headstone or marker and ongoing care of the grave are provided at no cost to the family. Funeral home charges aren't included. My guide to [burial insurance for veterans](/burial-insurance/veterans/) goes into more detail.

**State and county help.** Many states, counties and cities help pay for a basic burial or cremation when the person who died, and sometimes the family, can't afford it. Every program sets its own income and asset limits, its own dollar limits, and its own deadlines. Some won't help if you've already held the funeral, so call before you commit to anything. Start with the county's human services or social services office where the person died, or ask the funeral home which local programs it works with.

**Disasters and crimes.** FEMA's funeral help is generally limited to deaths caused by a federally declared disaster. If the death resulted from a crime, your state's crime victim compensation program may cover some funeral costs.

**Whole-body donation.** A medical school or donation program may pay for cremation after it has used the body. Most require the person to register before death, and they can turn a donation down, so I wouldn't count on this as your only plan.

The FTC's [Funeral Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-funeral-rule) also gives you rights that a lot of people don't know they have:

- **Call around.** Get prices by phone and compare at least two or three funeral homes.
- **Buy only what you want.** Apart from anything your state's law requires, a funeral home can't make you buy a package.
- **Buy the casket or urn somewhere else.** The funeral home can't charge you a fee for using one you bought elsewhere.
- **Skip the casket for cremation.** A simple alternative container is all direct cremation needs.
- **Skip embalming in most cases.** A funeral home can't tell you embalming is required by law when it isn't, and it needs your permission before charging for it.

## What happens if nobody can pay?

A funeral home doesn't have to take a case if no one can pay. When the estate and the family truly can't cover a burial or cremation, the county or city where the person died usually arranges a basic one. It's sometimes called an indigent burial.

Here's what you need to understand: the family usually has very little say. The local government picks the method, often cremation, and the place, such as a public cemetery. There may not be a service. Some counties want relatives to show they can't afford to pay before they step in, and the timelines depend on state and local law. If this is where you are, call the county's human services office or the medical examiner's or coroner's office as soon as you can. Waiting only narrows your options.

## Don't let this happen to your family

If you're reading this because someone you love died without life insurance, you already know how difficult it can be to find money for a funeral.

But what would happen if your family had to pay for your funeral tomorrow?

Would they have enough money readily available, or would they be searching for the same answers you're looking for today?

Planning ahead gives you the opportunity to make sure your family never has to face this financial burden.

## How to pay for your own funeral without life insurance

If you don't want to buy life insurance, you've really got two choices: save the money yourself, or prepay a funeral home.

**Save it yourself.** Open a separate account just for your funeral and name a payable-on-death beneficiary, so the money goes straight to that person without waiting for probate. You stay in control of the money. The catch is that your family only gets what you've managed to save by the day you die.

**Prepay a funeral home.** A preneed contract locks in your arrangements now and pays for them in advance. Before you sign one, the FTC suggests asking whether the prices are guaranteed, whether you can cancel and get a refund, whether the plan moves with you if you move, and what happens if the funeral home closes. State protections vary. My guide to [prepaid funerals](/prepaid-funeral/) covers the pros and cons.

The third option is a final expense life insurance policy, a small whole life policy that pays cash to the person you name. They can use it at any funeral home and for any final bill. I'll explain below when that makes more sense than saving.

Whichever way you go, write down your wishes and tell your family where to find your paperwork. My [12-step final planning guide](/12-step-final-planning-guide/) walks you through it.

The problem isn't saving money. The problem is whether you'll live long enough to save enough money. At $50 a month, your family has $600 after the first year and $3,000 after five years. If you die before you reach your goal, they have to come up with the rest.

That's the gap first-day life insurance fills. With a first-day policy, the full death benefit is there from the day your coverage starts,* whether you die in the first month or twenty years later. Your family isn't depending on how much you managed to save.

## Planning for a funeral on a fixed income

On a fixed income, saving a few thousand dollars can take years, and one unexpected bill can wipe it out. That's why a policy with a level monthly premium is often easier to manage than a savings goal. But be honest with yourself about the amount. Pick a premium you can keep paying, because a policy that lapses when you stop paying can leave your family with nothing.

If you're on Medicaid or SSI, how you set the money aside matters:

- SSI doesn't count burial spaces, such as a plot, vault or headstone, as resources.
- It also doesn't count up to $1,500 in money set aside for burial and kept separate. That $1,500 is reduced by the face value of certain life insurance and by any [irrevocable burial arrangements](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-416.1231).
- Medicaid rules follow each state's plan.

My guides to [Medicaid and life insurance](/final-expense-life-insurance-medicaid/) and [Medicaid spend-down rules](/medicaid-spend-down-rules-on-life-insurance/) explain how coverage fits within those limits.

## When final expense insurance makes sense

Here's how I'd think about it. Final expense insurance makes sense when you don't have enough set aside for a funeral, you want your family to get money they can use right away at any funeral home, and you'd rather pay a set amount each month than hope your savings grow fast enough.

It doesn't make sense if you've already saved enough, or if you have other life insurance that would cover your funeral. And it doesn't make sense if the premium would strain your budget. I'd rather you buy a smaller policy you can keep than a bigger one you'll drop.

## What if you have health problems?

Health problems don't automatically mean you'll have to wait for coverage. Many people with common conditions still qualify for first-day coverage. It depends on the insurance company's health questions and how you answer them, which is exactly why it pays to compare more than one company. My [first-day coverage guide](/burial-insurance/with-first-day-coverage/) explains more, and my [health question search](/life-insurance-health-questions/) lets you see the questions insurance companies ask before you apply.

If you don't qualify for first-day coverage, there are two other types of policy:

- **Graded benefit.** If you die of natural causes in the first years, the policy pays a reduced amount. The benefit increases to the full amount over time.
- **Guaranteed acceptance.** Guaranteed-acceptance life insurance doesn't ask health questions, and you can't be turned down because of your health as long as you meet the policy's eligibility requirements. These policies are commonly available to people ages 50 through 85, although the exact age limits depend on the insurance company. Most have a waiting period, set by the policy's contract, before the full benefit is paid for a death from natural causes. During that time, your beneficiary usually gets back the premiums you paid plus interest or a set percentage, as the policy states.

I'd look at guaranteed acceptance only after you've found out whether you qualify for something better. My guides to [guaranteed issue life insurance for seniors](/burial-insurance/guaranteed-issue-life-insurance-for-seniors/) and [life insurance with no waiting period](/burial-insurance/life-insurance-with-no-waiting-period/) explain the differences.

## Bottom line

If someone just died and there's no insurance and no money, cut the cost first, then use what they left behind and the benefits they earned. Borrow last. Get the prices before anyone signs, and if nobody can pay, call the county early.

If you're planning ahead, be realistic about how long saving will take. If your savings won't get there in time, a small first-day policy can cover the difference, so your family never has to read an article like this one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there free burial insurance?

No. There's no free burial insurance. Some groups can get real government life insurance, such as VA life insurance for service members and veterans, group life insurance for most federal employees, and Wisconsin's State Life Insurance Fund for its residents. The amounts and rules vary by program.

### Can a funeral home ask for payment before the service?

Yes. The FTC's Funeral Rule doesn't cover when or how you pay, so each funeral home sets its own payment and deposit policy. Ask about it when you ask for the price list.

### Are funeral expenses tax deductible?

Not on your personal income tax return. The IRS lists [burial and funeral expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p529) as nondeductible.

### Can I buy a burial insurance policy for my parent?

In most cases, yes, as long as your parent agrees and signs the application. My guide to [buying burial insurance on someone else](/burial-insurance/on-someone-else/) explains how it works.
