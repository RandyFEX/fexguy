## Ways to pay for a funeral without life insurance

A funeral without life insurance is usually paid from several sources, and they differ sharply in reliability. Money the person left behind can be counted on; donations depend on other people, and borrowing creates new debt. The sources below run from most to least dependable. Each works better once the arrangements have been priced and [reduced to what the family can afford](#how-to-reduce-funeral-expenses).

### The deceased person's own money

The most dependable funeral money is money the person left behind, but only if the family can reach it in time.

Bank accounts with a payable-on-death beneficiary, and joint accounts with a surviving co-owner, usually pass directly to that person and can often be used within days. An account in the deceased's name alone is different. It generally becomes part of the estate, and someone may need to be appointed executor or use the state's small-estate procedure before the bank will release it, which can take weeks. Some funeral homes will wait to be paid from the estate; others won't. Ask before you sign.

If you're the named beneficiary of the person's IRA or 401(k), you don't owe the [10% early-withdrawal tax](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-exceptions-to-tax-on-early-distributions) on money you take out after the owner's death, even if you're under 59½. Regular income tax still applies, and the account company may need time to process the claim.

### Life insurance you didn't know about

A family that believes there's no life insurance may still find some. Coverage may exist through a current or former employer, a union, a credit union, a fraternal organization or an old individual policy. Bank statements showing regular payments to an insurance company are one of the best clues.

State insurance regulators also make a free [NAIC Life Insurance Policy Locator](https://eapps.naic.org/life-policy-locator/) available for searching after a death. Results aren't immediate, so start early rather than count on it for this week's bill. If a policy does turn up, some funeral homes will accept an assignment of the benefit as payment.

### Contributions from family

Dividing the cost among relatives works when everyone agrees on the arrangements and the budget before anything is signed. Its failure point is the contract: the person who signs carries the legal obligation, and other relatives' promises to contribute don't change that. Agree in writing on who signs and how much each person pays.

### Crowdfunding

Crowdfunding can help families raise money for funeral expenses, but there is no guarantee that donations will cover the bill. The amount raised depends largely on the family's social network, the financial resources of potential donors and how quickly contributions can be collected. Platforms may also deduct fees.

I consider crowdfunding a supplemental source of funeral funding rather than a dependable financial plan. It shouldn't determine what you agree to spend.

### Charitable and community assistance

Churches, employers and community organizations sometimes help with funeral costs, directly or by organizing a collection. The amount varies widely and is rarely known in advance, so, like crowdfunding, it can supplement a plan but shouldn't set the budget.

### Government benefits

Social Security, veterans and state or county benefits can reduce the bill for those who qualify, but most pay part of the cost, not all of it. See the [next section](#government-funeral-assistance-programs).

### Funeral home payment plans and personal borrowing

A funeral home payment plan, credit card or personal loan provides certainty and speed: the funeral proceeds on schedule. The cost is that the funeral becomes debt, usually with interest, that a family member carries long after the service. I wouldn't borrow until the other sources have been exhausted and the arrangements reduced to what the family can afford. With a payment plan, get the total cost and the payment schedule in writing.

## Government funeral assistance programs

Government funeral assistance falls into two categories. Social Security and VA benefits pay fixed amounts to eligible survivors. State and county programs are generally designed to provide a basic burial or cremation for people with insufficient resources, not to fund the arrangements a family would otherwise choose. Both are worth pursuing when someone dies without money, but I wouldn't rely on either as a funeral funding strategy.

### Social Security death payment

Social Security pays a one-time [$255](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-404.390) death payment. It goes to the surviving spouse if they were living together. If there's no such spouse, it goes to a spouse or child who was already receiving benefits on the person's record. No one else can receive it, it isn't paid to the funeral home, and the application must be filed within two years. If you qualify, claim it, but it covers only a small fraction of any funeral.

### Veterans burial benefits

Veterans benefits are worth checking whenever the person served. Discharge papers, often called a DD-214, are usually needed.

VA pays [burial allowances](https://www.va.gov/burials-memorials/veterans-burial-allowance/) to eligible survivors:

- For a death caused by military service, up to $2,000 for deaths on or after September 11, 2001.
- For other deaths on or after October 1, 2026, up to $1,032 toward burial and $1,032 toward a plot, when the veteran meets VA's eligibility rules. For deaths from October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026, the amounts are $1,002 each.

VA usually pays the allowance as a reimbursement after the funeral, so the family still needs a way to pay the bill first.

Burial in a VA national cemetery can be worth more than the allowance. Veterans who didn't receive a dishonorable discharge, and certain family members, can generally be buried there. The gravesite (where space is available), opening and closing the grave, a burial liner, a government headstone or marker and ongoing care of the grave are provided at no cost to the family. Funeral home charges aren't included. My guide to [burial insurance for veterans](/burial-insurance/veterans/) covers these benefits in more detail.

### State and county burial assistance

Many states, counties and cities help pay for a basic burial or cremation when the person who died, and in some programs the family, can't afford it. Each program sets its own income and asset limits, dollar limits and deadlines, and some limit the type or total cost of the funeral they'll help pay for. Some won't assist after the funeral has already taken place, so contact the program before committing to arrangements.

These programs generally pay part of the cost of a basic funeral, usually to the funeral home. They're different from the arrangements a county makes when no one is able to pay at all, which are covered [below](#what-happens-if-nobody-can-afford-the-funeral). The county's human services or social services office where the person died is the place to start, and many funeral homes know which local programs they work with.

### Disaster and crime victim assistance

FEMA's funeral assistance is generally limited to deaths caused by a federally declared disaster. If a death resulted from a crime, the state's crime victim compensation program may cover some funeral costs.

Cost reduction produces no money, but it lowers the amount every other source has to cover, and the decisions that matter are made before the contract is signed.

### Compare prices and get them itemized

The FTC's [Funeral Rule](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-453) requires funeral homes to give prices over the phone and to provide an itemized General Price List when you meet in person to discuss arrangements. Call at least two or three funeral homes before choosing one; prices for the same arrangements can differ within the same area.

### Required and optional charges

A funeral home may charge a basic services fee that can't be declined. Beyond that, and apart from anything your state's law requires, you can choose only the goods and services you want rather than a package. A funeral home can't tell you embalming is required by law when it isn't, and it needs your permission before charging for it. The FTC's [compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-funeral-rule) describes these protections in detail.

### Direct cremation and immediate burial

Direct cremation and immediate burial are the two lowest-cost arrangements most funeral homes offer. Both skip embalming and the viewing, and direct cremation doesn't require a casket: an alternative container is sufficient. A memorial service can still be held later at a church, a family home or another location, without the funeral home's facility and staff charges. My guides to [cremation costs](/cremation-cost-and-info/) and [burial versus cremation](/burial-vs-cremation/) compare the two in more detail.

### Buy merchandise elsewhere

You can buy a casket or urn from another seller, and the funeral home can't charge a fee for using one you bought elsewhere. For families who want a traditional burial, this can be one of the larger savings available.

### Whole-body donation

Some medical schools and donation programs cover cremation after the body has been used. Most require registration before death and can decline a donation, so this is an option to arrange in advance rather than a fallback for a family under time pressure.

## Who is financially responsible for funeral expenses?

Three questions are often confused: who has the authority to arrange the funeral, who is personally liable for the bill, and what the estate pays. The answers come from state law and contract law, not from a single federal rule.

### Authority to arrange the funeral

Each state sets its own order of who controls the arrangements. Many states start with a person the deceased named in writing, then the spouse, adult children, parents and siblings, but the order and who qualifies differ by state. In some states, the person with that authority can also be responsible for the reasonable cost, so check your state's rule.

### Signing the funeral home contract

A funeral home contract is a personal agreement to pay. Whoever signs it, or otherwise agrees to pay, owes the funeral home, even if the estate later reimburses them. That's the reason to settle the budget and the source of the money before anyone signs.

### Payment from the estate

Funeral expenses can be paid from the deceased person's estate. In states that follow the Uniform Probate Code, reasonable funeral expenses are paid right after the costs of administering the estate and ahead of most other debts when there isn't enough to pay everyone. Other states set their own order. If the estate has no assets, there's nothing to reimburse the person who paid.

### Obligations of family members

The FTC says relatives generally don't have to pay a deceased person's debts out of their own money. There are exceptions, such as a co-signer, a surviving spouse in some states, or a state law that makes a relative responsible. If it's unclear what applies, the county probate court or a local attorney can answer the question before you sign anything.

## What happens if nobody can afford the funeral?

A funeral home isn't required to accept a case when no one can pay. When neither the estate nor the family can cover a burial or cremation, the county or city where the person died generally arranges a basic disposition, sometimes called an indigent burial.

These arrangements follow the local agency's policies. The agency generally determines the method and location, which may be cremation or burial in a public cemetery, and a service may not be included. Some agencies require relatives to show they can't pay, and state and local law sets how long the agency waits for the family before proceeding. If this is the situation, contact the county's human services office, or the medical examiner's or coroner's office, as early as possible.

## How to pay for your own funeral without life insurance

Planning for your own funeral changes the question from where the money will come from to whether it will be there. Without life insurance, the two main approaches are personal savings and a prepaid funeral arrangement.

### Personal savings

Paying for your funeral with personal savings is a perfectly reasonable alternative to life insurance if you've already accumulated enough money and those funds will remain available to your family.

Both conditions are harder to meet than they appear. Money set aside for a funeral has to survive everything else that happens before death: medical bills, home repairs, help for family members and ordinary emergencies. It also has to be reachable. An account with a payable-on-death beneficiary goes directly to that person without waiting for probate; an account in your name alone may not be available until the estate is settled. Funeral prices also tend to rise over time, so an amount that's sufficient today may not be later.

If you receive SSI, up to $1,500 set aside for burial and kept separate isn't counted as a resource, and burial spaces such as a plot, vault or headstone aren't counted at all. That $1,500 is reduced by the face value of certain life insurance and by any [irrevocable burial arrangements](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-416.1231). Medicaid rules follow each state's plan; my guides to [Medicaid and life insurance](/final-expense-life-insurance-medicaid/) and [Medicaid spend-down rules](/medicaid-spend-down-rules-on-life-insurance/) explain how these limits apply.

### Prepaid funeral arrangements

A preneed contract selects your arrangements in advance and pays for them now, with a specific funeral home. Its strength is that the decisions are made and the money is committed. Its limitations come from the contract itself. Before signing, the FTC suggests asking whether the prices are guaranteed, whether you can cancel and receive a refund, whether the plan can be transferred if you move, and what happens if the funeral home closes. State protections vary. My guide to [prepaid funerals](/prepaid-funeral/) covers the advantages and drawbacks in more detail.

Whichever approach you choose, write down your wishes and tell your family where to find the paperwork. My [12-step final planning guide](/12-step-final-planning-guide/) walks through the process.

## Saving money vs. buying life insurance

Saving for your funeral is a perfectly reasonable financial strategy if you live long enough to fully fund it. Life insurance addresses the financial risk of dying before that happens. The calculator shows how long that period lasts.

At $50 a month, your family would have $600 after one year and $3,000 after five. A death in that period leaves the rest of the bill unfunded.

Life insurance removes that exposure by transferring the risk to an insurance company in exchange for a premium. With a first-day policy, the full death benefit is available from the day coverage starts,* whether death occurs in the first month or many years later.

Insurance isn't the less expensive choice in every case. Someone who lives a long time can pay more in premiums than the policy pays out, and savings remain available if plans change. What insurance buys is certainty about the amount your family receives, not a lower total cost. For some people the practical answer is a combination: a smaller policy to cover the immediate risk, with savings building alongside it. My [burial insurance calculator](/burial-insurance-calculator/) helps estimate how much coverage would be needed, and my guide to [what final expense insurance costs](/how-much-does-final-expense-insurance-cost/) explains how premiums are set.

## When life insurance is the better option

Life insurance is the better option when you don't have enough accessible money for your funeral and couldn't accumulate it quickly. It also pays a set amount directly to your beneficiary, usable at any funeral home, without waiting for an estate to be settled. It isn't necessary if accessible savings or other life insurance already cover the cost.

### Affordability and keeping the policy in force

A policy protects your family only while it stays in force, so the premium has to fit your budget for life, particularly on a fixed income. Final expense policies generally have level premiums, which makes them easier to plan around than a savings goal, but a policy that lapses because the premium became unaffordable can leave the family with nothing. I'd rather see someone keep a smaller policy for life than buy a larger one they can't maintain.

### If you have health problems

Health problems don't automatically mean a waiting period. Many people with common conditions qualify for first-day coverage; whether they do depends on each insurance company's health questions, which differ, so comparing companies matters. My [first-day coverage guide](/burial-insurance/with-first-day-coverage/) explains more, and my [health question search](/life-insurance-health-questions/) shows the questions insurance companies ask before you apply.

If first-day coverage isn't available, there are two other types of policy:

- **Graded benefit.** If death from natural causes occurs in the first years, the policy pays a reduced amount. The benefit increases to the full amount over time.
- **Guaranteed acceptance.** Guaranteed-acceptance life insurance doesn't ask health questions, and you can't be turned down because of your health as long as you meet the policy's eligibility requirements. These policies are commonly available to people ages 50 through 85, although the exact age limits depend on the insurance company. Most have a waiting period, set by the policy's contract, before the full benefit is paid for a death from natural causes. During that time, your beneficiary usually gets back the premiums you paid plus interest or a set percentage, as the policy states.

Guaranteed acceptance should come after you've determined whether you qualify for something better. ## Bottom line

After a death without insurance, the reliable money is what the person left behind and any coverage not yet found; government benefits, family, crowdfunding and charity are partial or uncertain, and borrowing comes last. Lowering the cost before anyone signs is what makes the rest of the plan workable.

For your own funeral, accessible savings that already cover the cost make insurance unnecessary. Until they do, an affordable policy you can keep is what guarantees the money is there.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there free burial insurance?

No. There's no free burial insurance. Some groups can get real government life insurance, such as VA life insurance for service members and veterans, group life insurance for most federal employees, and Wisconsin's State Life Insurance Fund for its residents. The amounts and rules vary by program.

### Can a funeral home ask for payment before the service?

Yes. The FTC's Funeral Rule doesn't cover when or how you pay, so each funeral home sets its own payment and deposit policy. Ask about it when you request the price list.

### Are funeral expenses tax deductible?

Not on your personal income tax return. The IRS lists [burial and funeral expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p529) as nondeductible.

### Can I buy a burial insurance policy for my parent?

In most cases, yes, as long as your parent agrees and signs the application. My guide to [buying burial insurance on someone else](/burial-insurance/on-someone-else/) explains how it works.
