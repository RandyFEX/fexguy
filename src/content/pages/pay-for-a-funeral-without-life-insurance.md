## What to do when someone dies without life insurance

You don't have to make every decision in the first few hours. Take these steps before you agree to anything:

1. **Don't sign a funeral home contract yet.** The person who signs is usually the one who owes the bill. Find out what the funeral will cost and where the money will come from first.
2. **Ask for prices.** Funeral homes must give you prices over the phone, and they must hand you an itemized General Price List when you meet in person to talk about arrangements. These rights come from the Federal Trade Commission's [Funeral Rule](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-453).
3. **Ask for the two lowest-cost options by name:** direct cremation and immediate burial. Both skip the viewing and ceremony.
4. **Ask about holding fees.** If you need a few days to gather money, ask what the funeral home charges per day to keep the body.
5. **Look for money the person left behind.** Check for bank accounts with a payable-on-death beneficiary, joint accounts, and life insurance from an employer, union, credit union or old policy. Bank statements showing premium payments are a good clue. State insurance regulators also run a free NAIC Life Insurance Policy Locator that searches for policies after a death.
6. **Find out whether the person served in the military.** Veterans may qualify for burial benefits (see below). Look for discharge papers, often called a DD-214.

## Who is responsible for paying for the funeral?

Two questions often get mixed up: who can make the arrangements, and who has to pay.

**Who makes the arrangements.** State law sets the order. It usually starts with a person the deceased named in writing, then the spouse, adult children, parents and siblings. Having the right to decide doesn't make you personally responsible for the cost.

**Who pays.** The deceased person's estate pays first. Many states let reasonable funeral expenses be paid ahead of most other debts of the estate. If the estate has no money, the bill falls on whoever signed the funeral home contract or agreed to pay.

In most cases, adult children and other relatives don't have to pay for a funeral just because they're family. State laws differ, so if you aren't sure, ask your county probate court or a local attorney before you sign.

## Ways to pay for a funeral with no money

Work through these options in this order. The first ones cost the least and the last ones cost the most.

1. **Choose a lower-cost arrangement.** Direct cremation or immediate burial, with a memorial service later, costs far less than a funeral with a viewing. This step can do more than any other to shrink the bill. See how to reduce funeral costs below.
2. **Use the person's own money.** Payable-on-death and joint accounts usually pass straight to the named person or co-owner. An account in the deceased's name alone may need an executor or a small-estate procedure before anyone can use it, which can take weeks. Some funeral homes will wait for the estate to pay. Others will accept a life insurance policy assigned to them as payment.
3. **Claim benefits.** Employer, union and fraternal life insurance, veterans benefits and the Social Security payment are covered in the next section.
4. **Share the cost among family.** Agree in writing on who signs and how much each person will contribute.
5. **Use an inherited retirement account.** If you're the named beneficiary of an IRA or 401(k), you don't owe the [10% early-withdrawal tax](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-exceptions-to-tax-on-early-distributions) on money you take out after the owner's death, even if you're under 59½. Regular income tax still applies, and the account company may need time to process the transfer.
6. **Ask friends and the community.** Churches, employers and online fundraisers can help. How much a fundraiser brings in, and how fast, is hard to predict, and online platforms may deduct fees.
7. **Ask the funeral home about a payment plan.** Terms vary from one funeral home to the next. Get the total cost and the payment schedule in writing.
8. **Borrow as a last resort.** Credit cards and personal loans can cover the bill quickly, but interest can keep the debt with you long after the funeral.

## Government and funeral financial assistance

Help exists, but most of it is limited, and some of it is only for certain people or places.

**Social Security lump-sum death payment.** Social Security pays a one-time [$255](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-404.390) to the deceased person's surviving spouse if they lived together. If there's no such spouse, it goes to a spouse or child who was already getting benefits on the person's record. Nobody else can receive it, and it isn't paid to the funeral home. You must apply within two years.

**Veterans burial benefits.** VA pays [burial allowances](https://www.va.gov/burials-memorials/veterans-burial-allowance/) to eligible survivors:

- For a death caused by military service, up to $2,000 for deaths on or after September 11, 2001.
- For other deaths on or after October 1, 2026, up to $1,032 toward burial and $1,032 toward a plot, when the veteran meets VA's eligibility rules. For deaths from October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026, the amounts are $1,002 each.
- VA usually pays these allowances back to you after the funeral.
- Many veterans can also be buried in a VA national cemetery with a government headstone or marker at no cost to the family. See our guide to [burial insurance for veterans](/burial-insurance/veterans/).

**State and county burial assistance.** Many states, counties and cities help pay for a basic burial or cremation when the person who died, and sometimes the family, can't afford it. Each program sets its own:

- income and asset limits;
- dollar limits on the help and on the total funeral bill;
- application deadlines.

Payment usually goes to the funeral home, and some programs require you to apply before the funeral takes place. To find out what's available where the person died, call the county's human services or social services office, or ask the funeral home which local programs it works with.

**Disaster and crime-related help.** FEMA's funeral help is generally limited to deaths caused by a federally declared disaster. If a death resulted from a crime, your state's crime victim compensation program may cover some funeral costs.

**Whole-body donation.** A medical school or donation program may cover cremation after it has used the body. Most programs require the person to register before death, and they can turn a donation down, so don't count on this as the only plan.

These medians don't include cemetery costs such as the plot, opening and closing the grave, or a headstone. Prices also vary a lot by area. Our [funeral cost map](/funeral-cost-map/) shows how costs compare from state to state, and our guide to [how much a funeral costs](/how-much-does-a-funeral-cost/) explains each charge.

The FTC's [Funeral Rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-funeral-rule) gives you rights that can lower the bill:

- **Get prices by phone** before you visit, and compare at least two or three funeral homes.
- **Buy only what you want.** Apart from items your state's law requires, a funeral home can't make you buy a package.
- **Bring your own casket or urn.** You can buy them anywhere, and the funeral home can't charge you a fee for using one you bought elsewhere.
- **Use an alternative container for direct cremation.** You don't need a casket for cremation.
- **Skip embalming in most cases.** A funeral home can't tell you embalming is required by law when it isn't, and it needs your permission before charging for it.

The biggest savings come from simpler arrangements. Direct cremation or immediate burial, followed by a memorial service at a church, home or park, avoids most of the funeral home's service charges. Our guides to [cremation costs](/cremation-cost-and-info/) and [burial versus cremation](/burial-vs-cremation/) compare the choices.

## What happens if nobody can pay?

A funeral home doesn't have to take a case if no one can pay. When the person's estate and family truly can't cover a burial or cremation, the county or city where the person died usually arranges a basic one. This is sometimes called an indigent burial.

The family often has little say in what happens. The local government chooses the method, which is often cremation, and the place, such as a public cemetery. There may be no service. Some counties ask relatives to show they can't afford to pay before they step in. How long a county waits for family to claim a body, and what it does next, depends on state and local law. If you're in this situation, call the county's human services office or the medical examiner's or coroner's office as soon as you can.

## Don't let this happen to your family

If you're reading this because someone you love died without life insurance, you already know how difficult it can be to find money for a funeral.

But what would happen if your family had to pay for your funeral tomorrow?

Would they have enough money readily available, or would they be searching for the same answers you're looking for today?

Planning ahead gives you the opportunity to make sure your family never has to face this financial burden.

## How to pay for your own funeral without life insurance

There are three main ways to set money aside for your own funeral:

- **Save in a dedicated account.** Name a payable-on-death beneficiary so the money goes straight to that person without waiting for probate. You keep control of the money, but it only covers what you've saved by the time of your death.
- **Prepay with a funeral home.** A preneed contract chooses your arrangements now and pays for them in advance. Before you sign, the FTC suggests asking whether prices are guaranteed, whether you can cancel or get a refund, whether the plan transfers if you move, and what happens if the funeral home closes. State protections vary. Our guide to [prepaid funerals](/prepaid-funeral/) covers the pros and cons.
- **Buy final expense life insurance.** A small whole life policy pays cash to the person you name, who can use it at any funeral home and for any final bill.

Whatever you choose, write down your wishes and tell your family where to find your paperwork. Our [12-step final planning guide](/12-step-final-planning-guide/) walks you through it.

## Saving money vs. buying life insurance

The weakness of saving is the time it takes. If you save $50 a month, your family has $600 after the first year and $3,000 after five years. If you die before you reach your goal, they have to cover the rest.

That gap is what first-day life insurance coverage fills. With a first-day policy, the full death benefit is available from the day the coverage starts,* whether you die in the first month or many years later. Your family doesn't have to wait for savings to grow.

Insurance isn't always cheaper than saving. If you live a long time, the premiums you pay over the years can add up to more than the death benefit. Savings also stay yours to use if plans change. Many people do both: a smaller policy for protection now, and savings on top. Our [burial insurance calculator](/burial-insurance-calculator/) helps you estimate how much coverage you'd need, and our guide to [what final expense insurance costs](/how-much-does-final-expense-insurance-cost/) explains how premiums are set.

## Planning for a funeral on a fixed income

On a fixed income, saving a few thousand dollars can take many years, and an unexpected expense can wipe it out. A policy with a level monthly premium can be easier to budget for than a savings goal, as long as you choose an amount you can keep paying. A policy that lapses because you stopped paying can leave your family with nothing.

If you receive Medicaid or SSI, how you set money aside matters:

- SSI doesn't count burial spaces, such as a plot, vault or headstone, as resources.
- It also doesn't count up to $1,500 in money set aside for burial and kept separate. That $1,500 is reduced by the face value of certain life insurance and by any [irrevocable burial arrangements](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-416.1231).
- Medicaid rules follow each state's plan.

Our guides to [Medicaid and life insurance](/final-expense-life-insurance-medicaid/) and [Medicaid spend-down rules](/medicaid-spend-down-rules-on-life-insurance/) explain how coverage fits within these limits.

## When final expense insurance makes sense

Final expense insurance makes the most sense when:

- you don't have enough money set aside to cover a funeral;
- you want your family to receive money they can use right away, at any funeral home;
- you'd rather pay a set monthly amount than hope your savings grow fast enough.

It may not be worth it if you already have enough saved or have other life insurance that would cover your funeral. It's also not worth it if the premium would strain your budget.

## What if you have health problems?

Health problems don't automatically mean you'll have to wait for coverage. Many people with common conditions still qualify for first-day coverage, depending on the insurance company's health questions and your answers. See our [first-day coverage guide](/burial-insurance/with-first-day-coverage/). You can also use our [health question search](/life-insurance-health-questions/) to see the questions insurance companies ask before you apply.

If you don't qualify for first-day coverage, there are two other types of policy:

- **Graded benefit.** The policy pays a reduced amount if you die of natural causes in the first years, increasing to the full amount over time.
- **Guaranteed acceptance.** Guaranteed-acceptance life insurance doesn't ask health questions, and you can't be turned down because of your health as long as you meet the policy's eligibility requirements. These policies are commonly available to people ages 50 through 85, although the exact age limits depend on the insurance company. Most have a waiting period, set by the policy's contract, before the full benefit is paid for a death from natural causes. During that time, your beneficiary usually gets back the premiums you paid plus interest or a set percentage, as the policy states.

Our guides to [guaranteed issue life insurance for seniors](/burial-insurance/guaranteed-issue-life-insurance-for-seniors/) and [life insurance with no waiting period](/burial-insurance/life-insurance-with-no-waiting-period/) explain the differences.

## Bottom line

When there's no life insurance and no money, put lower costs and the help you're entitled to ahead of borrowing. Know your rights before you sign, and call the county early if no one can pay.

If you're planning ahead, compare what your savings would be worth with what your family would need. If savings won't be enough in time, a small first-day policy can cover the difference.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there free burial insurance?

No. There is no free burial insurance. Some groups can get real government life insurance, such as VA life insurance for service members and veterans, group life insurance for most federal employees, and Wisconsin's State Life Insurance Fund for its residents. The amounts and rules vary by program.

### Can a funeral home ask for payment before the service?

Yes. The FTC's Funeral Rule doesn't cover when or how you pay, so each funeral home sets its own payment and deposit policy. Ask about it when you request the price list.

### Are funeral expenses tax deductible?

Not on your personal income tax return. The IRS lists [burial and funeral expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p529) as nondeductible.

### Can I buy a burial insurance policy for my parent?

In most cases, yes, as long as your parent agrees and signs the application. Our guide to [buying burial insurance on someone else](/burial-insurance/on-someone-else/) explains how it works.
