# One Last Amazing Adventure: The Most Beautiful Places to Scatter Ashes Along the Appalachian Trail

*Draft for Randy's review. Every fact below comes from A-master-research-dataset.csv and D-regulatory-verification-report.md. Anything marked "not established" stays that way until it is verified.*

## Quick answer

The Appalachian Trail runs through 14 states, and many of public agencies manage the land it crosses. There is no single rule for scattering ashes on the Trail. Shenandoah National Park, the Blue Ridge Parkway and the National Park Service's own Appalachian Trail lands allow it without a permit under strict conditions. Great Smoky Mountains National Park and Harpers Ferry require permission. New York State Parks do not allow it unless the state approves. For most national forests and state lands along the Trail, no published rule could be found, so the only safe answer is to ask the land manager first.

## Key points

- Every place in this guide is marked Permitted, Permit or permission required, Prohibited, or Unconfirmed.
- Silence is not permission. If no rule is published, call the land manager before you plan a ceremony.
- A beautiful place to remember someone is not always a place where ashes may be scattered. This guide keeps the two apart.
- National Park Service rules on the Trail require distance from trails and water: 100 feet in Shenandoah and on the Blue Ridge Parkway, and 100 yards on the Park Service's Appalachian Trail lands.
- Leave nothing behind: no urns, markers, plaques or containers.

## Why the Appalachian Trail

For a lot of people, the Appalachian Trail is more than a path. It is the hike they took with their father at twelve, the section they walked on their honeymoon, the trip they talked about for thirty years and finally made. It is fog lifting off a bald in June, a fire tower at the top of a long climb, a quiet pond after a day on rocky ground.

When someone who loved those places dies, it is natural to want to bring them back one more time. That is what this guide is about: one last amazing adventure, carried out with care, respect for the land, and respect for the rules that protect it for the next family.

If you are still deciding where a loved one's ashes should go, our guides to [scattering ashes in U.S. national parks](/scattering-ashes-in-us-national-parks/) and [scattering ashes from a cruise ship](/scattering-ashes-from-a-cruise-ship/) cover two other paths families often choose.

## Before you go: how scattering rules work on the Trail

The Appalachian Trail is a single footpath, but it is not a single piece of land. The National Park Service administers a protected corridor, and the Trail also passes through seven National Park Service units, six national forests, and many state parks, state forests, game lands and smaller public and private parcels. Whoever manages the ground under your feet sets the rule.

That is why one overlook can allow scattering while the summit a few miles away does not, or has no published rule at all.

The Appalachian Trail Conservancy, the nonprofit that coordinates care of the Trail, does not own most of this land and has not published a position on scattering ashes. Its Leave No Trace guidance asks hikers to leave natural and historical objects where they find them, which is a good guide for any ceremony.

The national forests along the Trail have not published rules on scattering ashes either, and some national forests elsewhere in the country say it is not authorized. That is why every national forest place in this guide is marked Unconfirmed, and why we give the ranger district to call.

### The four classifications in this guide

- **Permitted.** The land manager publishes conditions under which you may scatter without a permit. You still have to follow every condition.
- **Permit or permission required.** You need a permit, a letter, or approval before the ceremony.
- **Prohibited.** The land manager says it is not allowed, or not allowed without official approval.
- **Unconfirmed.** We could not find a published rule. That does not mean it is allowed. Call before you plan anything.

### Remembrance places and scattering places

Some of the most meaningful places on the Trail are summits with historic towers, famous rocks, or narrow ledges where any scattering rule would be impossible to meet. Those can still be perfect places to remember someone: to read a letter, share a story, take a photo, or sit quietly. In this guide we call them remembrance places. Where a land manager names a separate spot where ashes may be scattered, we tell you where it is.

### What the National Park Service rules ask of you

Where the Park Service allows scattering, the conditions are similar:

- The remains must be cremated and pulverized.
- Scatter on the ground, well away from trails, roads, buildings and water.
- Spread the ashes so they do not collect in one place.
- Leave nothing behind: no marker, urn or container.
- Large groups or formal ceremonies may need a separate permit.

The exact distances and steps differ by park, and they are listed with each destination below.

### Leave no trace

Even where scattering is allowed, the land comes first. Stay on durable ground, keep fragile plants like the grasses of the high balds untouched, carry out everything you carry in, and choose a quiet moment when other hikers are not nearby.

## Georgia

The Trail begins in the Chattahoochee-Oconee National Forest. We could not find a published Forest Service rule on scattering ashes for this forest, so every Georgia destination in this guide is Unconfirmed. Call the Toccoa Ranger District before you plan a ceremony.

### Springer Mountain

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

Springer Mountain is where the Appalachian Trail begins. A bronze plaque and the first white blaze mark the southern terminus at 3,782 feet. From the Forest Service Road 42 trailhead, the walk is about 2 miles round trip. For anyone who ever dreamed of hiking the whole Trail, there may be no more fitting place to start one last journey. Leave the plaque and blaze area exactly as you found it.

### Blood Mountain via the Byron Reece Trail

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

The highest point on the Georgia section of the Trail, Blood Mountain rises to 4,461 feet, with a historic stone shelter on the summit. The out-and-back from Byron Reece is about 4.3 miles with almost 1,500 feet of climbing; a longer loop via the Freeman Trail is about 6.2 miles and rated difficult. Treat the stone shelter as the historic structure it is. Between March 1 and June 1, campers between Jarrard Gap and Neel Gap must carry a hard-sided bear canister.

### Long Creek Falls from Three Forks

**Accessible · Unconfirmed**

A gentle walk of about 1.8 miles round trip follows the Trail along the water, then a short side trail leads to the falls. The road in, Forest Service Road 58, is rough. Families who want a shorter, softer day in the north Georgia woods often find this one fits.

## North Carolina

North Carolina's Trail destinations here are in the Pisgah and Nantahala National Forests. Neither forest has a published rule we could find, so each is Unconfirmed. The National Forests in North Carolina office and the Appalachian Ranger District are the right first calls.

### Max Patch

**Accessible · Unconfirmed**

Max Patch is an open, grassy bald with views in every direction, reached on about 1.6 miles round trip. It is one of the most loved places on the southern Trail, and its grasses are fragile, so stay on the path. Camping and fire restrictions have been in place since 2021.

### Wayah Bald Lookout Tower

**Accessible · Unconfirmed**

A paved path of about 0.3 miles leads from the parking area to a historic stone lookout tower, and the Appalachian Trail Conservancy describes the path as wheelchair accessible. For a family member who can no longer manage a mountain trail, this may be the most welcoming summit in the state. The final 4.5 miles of road are gravel, and seasonal closures have been reported.

### Lover's Leap at Hot Springs

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

From the Trail town of Hot Springs, the climb to Lover's Leap is about 2.6 miles round trip, with a shorter 1.6-mile loop from the Silvermine parking area. The Forest Service manages it as part of the Appalachian Ranger District.

## Tennessee and the Great Smoky Mountains

The Trail follows the Tennessee and North Carolina state line through Great Smoky Mountains National Park. The park allows scattering with a free Letter of Permission that you download from the park's website and carry with you. The park publishes two sets of distances. Its Letter of Permission (July 2026) asks you to stay 300 feet from buildings, towers, historic sites and cemeteries, 150 feet from roads, trails, campgrounds and picnic areas, and 150 feet from water. The park's own rulebook, the Superintendent's Compendium (updated June 2026), lists 200 feet, 100 feet and 150 feet for the same places. Following the letter's distances satisfies both, and the park can answer questions at (865) 436-1200. No birds, butterflies or other objects may be released, and a group of more than 25 people needs a Special Use Permit.

### Kuwohi (formerly Clingmans Dome)

**Accessible · Permit or permission required**

At 6,643 feet, Kuwohi is the highest point in the Smokies, and the Trail crosses the summit. The observation tower is on the National Register of Historic Places, so any scattering must be at least 300 feet from it. The tower and summit are a remembrance place; a scattering spot that meets the park's distances must be away from the tower, the paved path and the parking area. The park publishes coordinates for the Kuwohi parking area: 35.556209, -83.495269.

### Charlies Bunion

**Extraordinary · Permit or permission required**

The Park Service describes this hike from Newfound Gap as about 8 miles round trip on the Appalachian Trail. Charlies Bunion is a rock outcrop surrounded by steep cliffs. Be careful at the edge, and remember the park's 150-foot distance from the trail.

### Round Bald and Jane Bald from Carvers Gap

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

In the Roan Highlands on the state line, the Trail crosses open balds on a walk of about 2.6 miles round trip. The parking area fills quickly. The vegetation on the balds is fragile, so stay on the trail. We could not establish which agency manages this land, so ask before you plan anything.

### Laurel Fork Gorge and Falls

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

About 5 miles round trip from a Forest Service lot near Hampton, the Trail follows Laurel Fork through a gorge to a waterfall. The Laurel Fork Shelter burned in 2024, and the area is closed to camping.

## Virginia

Virginia holds more of the Trail than any other state, and three National Park Service units with clear, published rules.

**Shenandoah National Park** allows scattering without a permit: pulverized remains, on the ground at least 100 feet from any trail, road, developed facility or water, spread so they do not collect, no scattering from the air, and nothing left behind. A permit is needed for ceremonies or gatherings that might affect park operations. The park entrance fee applies. Call (540) 999-3500 with questions.

**The Blue Ridge Parkway** allows scattering without a permit for groups of 25 or fewer, pulverized, at least 100 feet from trails, roads, developed facilities and water. It is not allowed at Craggy Gardens, Devils Courthouse or Rough Ridge. Call 828 348-3400.

### Blackrock (Shenandoah)

**Accessible · Permitted**

From the Blackrock parking area at Skyline Drive mile 84.4, a short walk on the Trail leads to a rocky summit. The Appalachian Trail Conservancy lists the walk at about 1 mile round trip with about 160 feet of climbing. Scatter on the ground at least 100 feet from the trail.

### Bear Den Mountain (Shenandoah)

**Accessible · Permitted**

From a small parking area at Beagle Gap, Skyline Drive milepost 99.3, the Trail climbs to the open summit of Bear Den Mountain in the park's South District. The Appalachian Trail Conservancy lists the walk at about 2.2 miles round trip. Shenandoah's conditions apply.

### Compton Peak (Shenandoah)

**Accessible · Permitted**

From the Compton Gap parking area at Skyline Drive mile 10.4, the Trail climbs to Compton Peak in the park's North District, about 2.4 miles round trip. Shenandoah's conditions apply.

### Thunder Ridge Overlook (Blue Ridge Parkway)

**Accessible · Permitted**

At milepost 74, the overlook is part of the Appalachian Trail and looks out over the Arnold Valley. A short loop trail leaves from it. The Parkway's conditions apply. The wilderness area next to the overlook is not Park Service land.

### McAfee Knob

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

The Roanoke Appalachian Trail Club, which maintains it, describes the hike as just under 8 miles round trip, with about 1,400 feet of climbing. A pedestrian bridge over Route 311 opened in March 2025, and a shuttle runs from the I-81 Exit 140 Park and Ride. We could not establish who manages the land at the Knob, so this is a remembrance place until the manager confirms a rule.

### Grayson Highlands and Rhododendron Gap

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

From Massie Gap in Grayson Highlands State Park, the Rhododendron Trail reaches the Appalachian Trail in about half a mile. Continuing to Rhododendron Gap is about 5.2 miles round trip. Wild ponies graze the rocky balds; do not feed or touch them. Past the fence the land is Jefferson National Forest. Neither Virginia State Parks nor the national forest has a published rule we could find.

## West Virginia: Harpers Ferry

Harpers Ferry National Historical Park allows scattering only with a special use permit and only in three designated areas: the Shenandoah River shoreline at the Point, the Potomac River shoreline at Potoma Wayside, and the vegetation next to Jefferson Rock. The park does not give exact points, and neither do we. Scattering ashes is one of the activities the park lists as needing a special use permit. The park's permit page says a non-refundable $50 application fee is due when you apply, that applications should arrive at least one month before the event, and that processing can take up to 30 days. The permits office is at 304-535-6232.

### Harpers Ferry Lower Town and the Point

**Accessible · Permit or permission required**

The Trail runs right through Harpers Ferry. The historic Lower Town is a remembrance place. The Point, where the Shenandoah meets the Potomac, is the setting for one of the park's designated scattering areas, the Shenandoah River shoreline.

### Jefferson Rock

**Accessible · Permit or permission required**

The Trail passes Jefferson Rock between the Lower Town and Camp Hill. The rock itself is closed to climbing, standing and sitting, so it is a remembrance place. The vegetation beside it is one of the park's three designated scattering areas, with a permit.

## Maryland

Maryland's Department of Natural Resources says it is not legal in Maryland to spread ashes on public lands, so the Maryland state park destinations are remembrance places only. The nearby C&O Canal National Historical Park, run by the National Park Service, allows scattering with its free letter permit: notify the permits office in advance, receive a permit number, and stay at least 50 feet from the towpath, roads, trails and the Potomac River.

### Weverton Cliffs

**Extraordinary · Prohibited**

Weverton Cliffs is about 2 miles round trip with about 625 feet of climbing. Maryland DNR lists the overlook as part of South Mountain State Park, so it is a remembrance place. Below the cliffs, the Trail follows the C&O Canal towpath, where the canal park's letter permit applies.

### Washington Monument State Park

**Accessible · Prohibited**

A short walk of about 0.4 miles on the Trail reaches the park's stone monument, a historic structure. It is a peaceful remembrance place with a view over the valley.

### Gathland State Park at Crampton Gap

**Accessible · Prohibited**

About half a mile on the Trail, the War Correspondents Memorial Arch stands at Crampton Gap, a historic structure in a quiet park setting.

## Pennsylvania

### Bake Oven Knob

**Accessible · Unconfirmed**

A short, easy walk of about 0.8 miles round trip leads to a rocky knob at 1,560 feet. The land is reported to be State Game Lands 217, managed by the Pennsylvania Game Commission. Wear blaze orange during hunting season. We could not find a Game Commission rule on scattering ashes.

*Other Pennsylvania candidates (Table Rock, Wolf Rocks, Weathering Knob and the Boiling Springs town walk) are held until their land managers are confirmed.*

## New Jersey

We could not find a published New Jersey State Park Service rule on scattering ashes, so the New Jersey destinations are Unconfirmed.

### Sunfish Pond

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

A glacial pond in the Kittatinny Ridge, Sunfish Pond lies on the Trail in Worthington State Forest. From the Dunnfield Creek trailhead the Park Service lists the trail at about 3.4 to 3.5 miles each way, about 7 miles round trip, with about 966 feet of elevation change. Keep well back from the water.

### High Point Monument

**Accessible · Unconfirmed**

The obelisk at High Point marks the highest point in New Jersey, 1,803 feet. The Appalachian Trail Conservancy lists about 3 miles round trip to the viewing platform with about 300 feet of climbing, plus about half a mile more to the monument. The tower is closed in winter.

### Pochuck Boardwalk

**Accessible · Unconfirmed**

A flat walk of about 2 miles round trip on a long boardwalk through wetlands. The Appalachian Trail Conservancy notes that it is not certified as accessible under federal standards, and parking is along the shoulder of Route 517. It is a gentle choice for family members who cannot climb.

## New York

New York State Parks does not allow ashes to be released in state parks unless the Office of Parks, Recreation and Historic Preservation officially approves.

### Bear Mountain

**Accessible · Prohibited unless the state approves**

Perkins Memorial Drive leads close to the summit, with accessible parking along the road, and the Trail passes through the summit area. The drive is open April 1 through November 30, weather permitting. Bear Mountain is a beautiful remembrance place. Scattering here needs the state's approval first.

*Other New York candidates (Buchanan Mountain, Prospect Rock, Fitzgerald Falls and the Great Swamp boardwalk) are held until their land managers are confirmed.*

## Connecticut

We were not able to confirm the land manager or a scattering rule for any Connecticut destination in this research, so none is listed yet. Great Falls at Falls Village and Lions Head remain candidates for a future update.

## Massachusetts

### Mount Greylock

**Accessible · Unconfirmed**

The highest point in Massachusetts can be reached by the summit road, and the Trail crosses the top. The Veterans War Memorial Tower and Bascom Lodge are historic structures. For hikers, the state's suggested day hike is about 6.2 miles round trip with about 1,070 feet of climbing. Vehicles over 22 feet are not allowed on the road. We could not find a Department of Conservation and Recreation rule on scattering ashes.

## Vermont

### Thundering Falls

**Accessible · Unconfirmed**

A universally accessible route of about 0.6 miles round trip, with a 700-foot boardwalk and about 500 feet of gravel, leads to a view of the falls near Killington. Grades reach 7 percent on the boardwalk and 9 percent on the gravel, and part of the gravel can be wet. The Forest Service describes an accessible parking lot on River Road; the Appalachian Trail Conservancy says there are no designated accessible spaces, so check before you go. The land is Green Mountain National Forest, which has no published scattering rule we could find.

## New Hampshire

### Franconia Ridge

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

The Trail runs along an open, above-treeline ridge with long views in every direction. The classic loop from Lafayette Place in Franconia Notch State Park is about 8.9 miles and is rated a difficult, all-day hike. Weather on the ridge can turn severe. Who manages the ridge crest is not established, so this is a remembrance place until it is.

## Maine

The Trail ends at Baxter State Park. We could not find a published Baxter rule on scattering ashes either way. Contact the park before you plan anything.

### Katahdin via the Hunt Trail

**Extraordinary · Unconfirmed**

The northern end of the Appalachian Trail. Baxter State Park lists the Hunt Trail at about 10.4 miles round trip with about 4,188 feet of climbing, taking 8 to 12 hours. This is a serious mountain hike, and the trail closes for winter, roughly mid-October to late May. For a hiker who finished the Trail here, or always meant to, it is the summit that says "you made it."

### Little and Big Niagara Falls

**Accessible · Unconfirmed**

A gentler Baxter walk of about 2.6 miles round trip along Nesowadnehunk Stream leads to two waterfalls. It is a good choice for family members who want to be near the end of the Trail without the summit climb.

## Planning the day

- **Call first.** Use the contact listed for each land manager. Ask whether scattering is allowed, what distances apply, and whether your group size needs a permit.
- **Choose your group size carefully.** Several parks set a limit of 25 people before a permit is required.
- **Prepare the remains.** National Park Service rules require cremated remains to be pulverized. Ask your funeral home or crematory if you are unsure.
- **Bring a scattering container you will carry home.** Nothing may be left behind.
- **Watch the wind and the water.** Stand so the wind carries ashes away from your group, and keep the required distance from streams, ponds and waterfalls.
- **Plan for the hike.** Check the weather, seasonal road closures and parking limits. Carry water, layers and a map.
- **Think about everyone in the group.** Several places in this guide, like Wayah Bald, Thundering Falls and the Pochuck Boardwalk, let older family members take part without a hard climb.

## A final word

A place on the Appalachian Trail can hold a whole life's worth of memories. Taking someone there one last time, and doing it in a way that respects the land and the people who care for it, is a gift to them and to every family who comes after you.
