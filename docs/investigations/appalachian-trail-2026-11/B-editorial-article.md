# One Last Amazing Adventure: The Most Beautiful Places to Scatter Ashes Along the Appalachian Trail

## Quick answer

The Appalachian Trail runs through 14 states, and many of public agencies manage the land it crosses. There is no single rule for scattering ashes on the Trail. Shenandoah National Park, the Blue Ridge Parkway and the National Park Service's own Appalachian Trail lands allow it without a permit under strict conditions. Great Smoky Mountains National Park and Harpers Ferry require permission. New York State Parks do not allow it unless the state approves. For most national forests and state lands along the Trail, no published rule could be found, so the only safe answer is to ask the land manager first.

## Key points

- Every place in this guide is marked Permitted, Permit or permission required, Prohibited, or Unconfirmed.
- Silence is not permission. If no rule is published, call the land manager before you plan a ceremony.
- The place where you say goodbye does not have to be the place where the ashes rest. Every destination has a remembrance plan and a scattering plan.
- National Park Service rules on the Trail require distance from trails and water: 100 feet in Shenandoah and on the Blue Ridge Parkway, and 100 yards on the Park Service's Appalachian Trail lands.
- Leave nothing behind: no urns, markers, plaques or containers.

## Why the Appalachian Trail

For a lot of people, the Appalachian Trail is more than a path. It is the hike they took with their father at twelve, the section they walked on their honeymoon, the trip they talked about for thirty years and finally made. It is fog lifting off a bald in June, a fire tower at the top of a long climb, a quiet pond after a day on rocky ground.

When someone who loved those places dies, you can take them back one more time. Carry their ashes securely sealed in an unbreakable urn, walk the Trail together, and stand at the summit, the waterfall or the overlook they loved. Tell the stories, read the letter, take the photographs and celebrate the life you came to honor. That is the heart of this guide: one last amazing adventure.

The place where you say goodbye does not have to be the place where the ashes rest. Many of the Trail's most spectacular places are not open to scattering, or have no published rule. For every destination, we show you how to hold a meaningful remembrance there, and where the ashes can be scattered legally: sometimes at the same spot, sometimes at a nearby park that allows it, and sometimes only after you ask the land manager.

If you are still deciding where a loved one's ashes should go, our guides to [scattering ashes in U.S. national parks](/scattering-ashes-in-us-national-parks/) and [scattering ashes from a cruise ship](/scattering-ashes-from-a-cruise-ship/) cover two other paths families often choose.

## Before you go: how scattering rules work on the Trail

The Appalachian Trail is a single footpath, but it is not a single piece of land. The National Park Service administers a protected corridor, and the Trail also passes through seven National Park Service units, six national forests, and many state parks, state forests, game lands and smaller public and private parcels. Whoever manages the ground under your feet sets the rule.

That is why one overlook can allow scattering while the summit a few miles away does not, or has no published rule at all.

The Appalachian Trail Conservancy, the nonprofit that coordinates care of the Trail, does not own most of this land and has not published a position on scattering ashes. Its Leave No Trace guidance asks hikers to leave natural and historical objects where they find them, which is a good guide for any ceremony.

The national forests along the Trail have not published rules on scattering ashes either, and some national forests elsewhere in the country say it is not authorized. That is why every national forest place in this guide is marked Unconfirmed, and why we give the ranger district to call.

### The four classifications in this guide

- **Permitted.** The land manager publishes conditions under which you may scatter without a permit. You still have to follow every condition.
- **Permit or permission required.** You need a permit, a letter, or approval before the ceremony.
- **Prohibited.** The land manager says it is not allowed, or not allowed without official approval.
- **Unconfirmed.** We could not find a published rule. That does not mean it is allowed. Call before you plan anything.

### Remembrance places and scattering places

Think of each trip in two parts. The remembrance is the adventure itself: the walk, the view, the words you share. The scattering is the moment the ashes are released, and it must happen where the land manager allows it. At a few places on the Trail, like the Shenandoah summits, the Blue Ridge Parkway and Great Smoky Mountains National Park, the two can happen together under the park's conditions. Everywhere else, keep your loved one's ashes sealed in an unbreakable urn during the ceremony and bring them back with you, then scatter them at a verified location or once the land manager has given permission.

### What the National Park Service rules ask of you

If your chosen destination permits ash scattering, the following guidelines will help you plan a respectful ceremony. Specific requirements vary by location.

- The remains must be cremated and pulverized.
- Scatter on the ground, well away from trails, roads, buildings and water.
- Spread the ashes so they do not collect in one place.
- Leave nothing behind: no marker, urn or container.
- Large groups or formal ceremonies may need a separate permit.

The exact distances and steps differ by park, and they are listed with each destination below.

### Leave no trace

Even where scattering is allowed, the land comes first. Stay on durable ground, keep fragile plants like the grasses of the high balds untouched, carry out everything you carry in, and choose a quiet moment when other hikers are not nearby.

## Tennessee and the Great Smoky Mountains

The Trail follows the Tennessee and North Carolina state line through Great Smoky Mountains National Park. The park allows scattering with a free Letter of Permission that you download from the park's website and carry with you. The park publishes two sets of distances. Its Letter of Permission (July 2026) asks you to stay 300 feet from buildings, towers, historic sites and cemeteries, 150 feet from roads, trails, campgrounds and picnic areas, and 150 feet from water. The park's own rulebook, the Superintendent's Compendium (updated June 2026), lists 200 feet, 100 feet and 150 feet for the same places. Following the letter's distances satisfies both, and the park can answer questions at (865) 436-1200. No birds, butterflies or other objects may be released, and a group of more than 25 people needs a Special Use Permit.

### Kuwohi (formerly Clingmans Dome)

**Accessible · Permit or permission required**

At 6,643 feet, Kuwohi is the highest point in the Smokies, and the Trail crosses the summit. A steep, paved path of about a mile round trip climbs to the observation tower, a historic structure on the National Register of Historic Places.

**Why this destination is accessible:** A paved path of about a mile round trip leads from the parking area to the observation tower at the highest point in the Smokies. The Park Service notes the path is steep.

**Your ceremony here:** Walk up together and hold your remembrance on the tower or the summit. A group of more than 25 people needs a Special Use Permit from the park. Kuwohi Road is closed in winter.

**Where to scatter:** Scattering is allowed in Great Smoky Mountains National Park with the park's free Letter of Permission: download it and carry it with you. Choose a spot away from the tower, the paved path and the parking area that meets the letter's distances: 300 feet from buildings, towers and historic sites, and 150 feet from roads, trails and water. The ashes must be pulverized, and nothing may be released or left behind.

## Virginia

Virginia holds more of the Trail than any other state, and three National Park Service units with clear, published rules.

**Shenandoah National Park** allows scattering without a permit: pulverized remains, on the ground at least 100 feet from any trail, road, developed facility or water, spread so they do not collect, no scattering from the air, and nothing left behind. A permit is needed for ceremonies or gatherings that might affect park operations. The park entrance fee applies. Call (540) 999-3500 with questions.

**The Blue Ridge Parkway** allows scattering without a permit for groups of 25 or fewer, pulverized, at least 100 feet from trails, roads, developed facilities and water. It is not allowed at Craggy Gardens, Devils Courthouse or Rough Ridge. Call 828 348-3400.

## West Virginia: Harpers Ferry

Harpers Ferry National Historical Park allows scattering only with a special use permit and only in three designated areas: the Shenandoah River shoreline at the Point, the Potomac River shoreline at Potoma Wayside, and the vegetation next to Jefferson Rock. The park does not give exact points, and neither do we. Scattering ashes is one of the activities the park lists as needing a special use permit. The park's permit page says a non-refundable $50 application fee is due when you apply, that applications should arrive at least one month before the event, and that processing can take up to 30 days. The permits office is at 304-535-6232.

### Harpers Ferry Lower Town and the Point

**Accessible · Permit or permission required**

The Trail runs right through Harpers Ferry, a historic town at the meeting of the Shenandoah and Potomac rivers. The walk from the visitor center to Lower Town on the park's trail is about 30 minutes to an hour each way, or ride the park shuttle.

**Why this destination is accessible:** The park's shuttle runs from the visitor center lot to Lower Town, and the Point is a short walk through the historic streets.

**Your ceremony here:** Walk the historic streets together, then gather at the Point, where the two rivers meet, for your words. A formal ceremony in the park needs a special use permit.

**Where to scatter:** This is one of the few places on the Trail with a designated scattering area: the Shenandoah River shoreline at the Point. Apply for the park's special use permit at least a month ahead; the park charges a non-refundable $50 application fee and can take up to 30 days to process it.

### Jefferson Rock

**Why this destination is accessible:** It sits just above Lower Town on the Trail, a short walk away, though the stone steps up to it are steep.

The verified options in this guide are farther south, such as C&O Canal National Historical Park, which allows scattering with its free letter permit: notify the permits office in advance at (301) 745-5815, receive a permit number, and stay at least 50 feet from the towpath, roads, trails and the Potomac River.

*Other Pennsylvania candidates (Table Rock, Wolf Rocks, Weathering Knob and the Boiling Springs town walk) are held until their land managers are confirmed.*

## Planning the day

- **Plan the remembrance and the scattering separately.** Decide where you will gather, and where the ashes will be scattered legally. Ask the land manager first wherever no rule is published, and keep the answer in writing.
- **Choose your group size carefully.** Several parks set a limit of 25 people before a permit is required.
- **Prepare the remains.** National Park Service rules require cremated remains to be pulverized. Ask your funeral home or crematory if you are unsure.
- **Bring a scattering container you will carry home.** Nothing may be left behind.
- **Watch the wind and the water.** Stand so the wind carries ashes away from your group, and keep the required distance from streams, ponds and waterfalls.
- **Plan for the hike.** Check the weather, seasonal road closures and parking limits. Carry water, layers and a map.
- **Think about everyone in the group.** Several places in this guide, like Wayah Bald, Thundering Falls and the Pochuck Boardwalk, let older family members take part without a hard climb.

## A final word

A place on the Appalachian Trail can hold a whole life's worth of memories. Taking someone there one last time, and doing it in a way that respects the land and the people who care for it, is a gift to them and to every family who comes after you.
